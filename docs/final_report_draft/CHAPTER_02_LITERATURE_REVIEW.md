## 2.1 Football training analysis and the limits of observation

Football coaching requires judgments about collective shape, individual movement and responses to instructions while play continues. A coach cannot inspect every player and interaction at once. In an observational study of elite youth coaches, Nicholls and Worsfold (2016) found that accuracy varied substantially across football actions and argued for objective performance analysis as an aid to observation. This does not make the coach's interpretation dispensable: practitioners in a professional academy wanted technical and tactical information that was meaningful, understandable and placed in its football context (Davidson *et al.*, 2024).

Social loafing was an early motivation for this project, but it is an unsuitable label for a video-derived player score. Latané, Williams and Harkins (1979) studied reduced individual effort in group tasks; the construct concerns motivation and social conditions, not a direct mapping from low displacement to unwillingness. A player may move little because of an assigned position, ball location, tactical discipline, fatigue or limited visibility. The defensible computer-vision target is therefore **observable movement and tactical response**, with interpretation left to coaches.

Existing approaches quantify different pieces of this problem. Wearable positioning and multi-camera optical systems estimate external load and trajectories, but their measurements require validity checks and can disagree by movement category (Pons *et al.*, 2019). Video and notational analysis can preserve events for later review, while tracking supplies spatial evidence. Neither a trajectory nor a transcript alone establishes whether a particular coaching instruction was followed. This project addresses that connection between spoken context and visible response.

## 2.2 Player detection and domain adaptation

Object detection locates players before tracking can associate them. The original YOLO formulation treated detection as a single regression problem, making real-time analysis practical (Redmon *et al.*, 2016). Later YOLO work refined the accuracy–speed trade-off through architecture and training choices (Wang, Bochkovskiy and Liao, 2023). Transformer detectors offer a different formulation: DETR uses set prediction and bipartite matching (Carion *et al.*, 2020), while RF-DETR develops a specialist real-time family intended to adapt efficiently to target datasets (Robinson *et al.*, 2026). These papers establish alternative detector designs; their published benchmark rankings cannot be assumed to transfer to a particular training video.

Football exposes that transfer problem sharply. Wide views make players small; floodlights and shadows change appearance; occlusion, motion blur and similar kits complicate localisation. SportsMOT identifies fast, variable movement and similar player appearance as central dataset properties (Cui *et al.*, 2023). SoccerNet-Tracking likewise reports difficulties with fast motion and severe occlusion in football footage (Cioppa *et al.*, 2022). Domain adaptation—fine-tuning on relevant imagery, selecting resolution and testing on held-out footage—is consequently more informative than simply choosing the newest detector. Tiling can preserve small-player detail in high-resolution frames, but also creates overlap and duplicate-detection risks that must be reconciled. Detection remains only the first stage: an accurately located box does not provide a durable player identity or a tactical interpretation.

## 2.3 Multi-object tracking, re-identification and persistent identity

Most practical multi-object trackers follow a tracking-by-detection pattern: detect objects in each frame, then associate new boxes with existing trajectories. SORT combines motion prediction and assignment efficiently, but its simple motion model has limited means of recovering an identity after a long occlusion or re-entry (Bewley *et al.*, 2016). Deep SORT adds a learned appearance distance, improving association when motion alone is ambiguous (Wojke, Bewley and Paulus, 2017). This illustrates a recurring trade-off: appearance can bridge gaps, yet it is only useful when the image retains enough distinctive detail.

ByteTrack tackles a different source of fragmentation. It associates lower-confidence detections with established tracks instead of discarding them automatically, potentially recovering partially occluded objects (Zhang *et al.*, 2022). BoT-SORT combines motion, appearance and camera-motion compensation (Aharon, Orfaig and Bobrovsky, 2022). On a fixed training camera, camera compensation may matter less than in broadcast footage, but close player interactions and missed detections still disrupt local association. These methods make different assumptions about confidence, motion and visual distinctiveness; they should be compared as complete pipelines under the same evaluation protocol.

Person re-identification (ReID) attempts to match appearances across disconnected observations. OSNet, for example, learns features at multiple spatial scales to improve person matching (Zhou *et al.*, 2019). The distinction between a **track ID** and a **verified player identity** remains essential. A tracker can assign a stable number for a short interval without proving that the number follows the same footballer across disappearance and return. Conversely, conservative matching may create multiple IDs for one player rather than make an unsafe merge. Both outcomes affect accumulated distance, speed and instruction-response claims.

Football makes generic ReID assumptions fragile. Teammates wear similar colours, distant crops contain few pixels, poses change rapidly, and occlusion can contaminate an appearance embedding. SoccerNet-Tracking's long-term component was introduced precisely because short clips do not capture all of these association failures (Cioppa *et al.*, 2022). Persistent identity must therefore be measured independently of detector quality. For this project, a player-specific conclusion requires evidence that the association survived the relevant time span; a plausible-looking track or a low switch count alone is insufficient.

These failure modes also complicate evaluation. A missed detection may terminate a track even when the association algorithm made no incorrect match. If the player reappears with a new ID, fragmentation grows; if the system reuses another player's ID, a false merge can contaminate every later player-specific observation. A method can reduce switches by avoiding difficult matches while increasing missed players. Thus association quality must be read alongside recall, false negatives and the number and length of resulting tracklets. This is why a detector comparison cannot stand in for an identity study, and why visual spot checks of a few frames are weaker evidence than an annotated temporal protocol.

## 2.4 Sports-specific tracking and re-entry

General pedestrian benchmarks do not fully represent football's motion, kit similarity and camera geometry. SportsMOT provides multi-sport sequences and shows that object association is a major difficulty even when detections are available (Cui *et al.*, 2023). SoccerNet-Tracking covers broadcast football, including long-term tracking (Cioppa *et al.*, 2022). SoccerTrack introduced full-pitch fish-eye and drone views with position data (Scott *et al.*, 2022); SoccerTrack v2 extends the full-pitch setting with panoramic matches and game-state annotations (Scott *et al.*, 2025). These resources are complementary rather than interchangeable: broadcast cameras frequently pan or cut, whereas a fixed smartphone camera trades stable viewpoint for smaller, more crowded players.

Sports-oriented methods respond to different failure modes. Deep-EIoU uses expanded spatial overlap and deep appearance features, reducing reliance on a linear motion predictor during irregular athletic movement (Huang *et al.*, 2024). Global Tracklet Association (GTA) revisits tracklets after local tracking, splitting identity-contaminated segments and linking compatible fragments (Sun *et al.*, 2024). GTATrack combines Deep-EIoU online association with global tracklet refinement for a SoccerTrack challenge setting (Jian *et al.*, 2026). In contrast, SRITrack is explicitly online and addresses broadcast re-entry with visibility screening and appearance-led association (Kao, Chou and Lin, 2026).

The comparison is methodological, not a transferable leaderboard. Online methods must decide with current and past evidence; offline linking can exploit a completed recording but risks joining different teammates who look alike. Re-entry logic designed around a moving broadcast boundary may behave differently in a fixed wide view where players disappear behind one another or leave a small frame. Similarly, a method's success on SportsMOT or SoccerTrack does not prove it will preserve identities in this project's footage. The literature justifies testing several complete detection–association–ReID combinations, including sports-specific alternatives, against the same frozen challenge clips and an explicit identity-safety criterion. It also justifies a product design that can continue with anonymous or team-level evidence when player-level association remains unsafe.

Dataset labels further shape what a benchmark can establish. Full-pitch resources may include jersey or position annotations unavailable to a system deliberately avoiding jersey-number identification; broadcast clips may offer a closer player view but discontinuous camera coverage. A fair comparison consequently needs the same source frames, ground truth, tracker reset policy and scoring rules for each candidate. Published sports-tracking results motivate the candidate methods, but the local evaluation must decide whether any candidate supports the stricter full-session use case.

## 2.5 Homography, calibration and spatial metrics

Image coordinates are not pitch distances. A planar homography maps points between the image and a pitch plane when sufficient corresponding landmarks and the relevant camera geometry are known. Sports-field registration commonly estimates such a mapping from field features (Chu *et al.*, 2022). TVCalib instead estimates camera parameters from pitch markings and minimises reprojection error, underscoring that field geometry and camera pose must be established rather than assumed (Theiner and Ewerth, 2023).

For tracked players, the bottom centre of a detection box is a practical approximation to the ground-contact point; it is not an automatically verified foot position. Projection then depends on the box coordinate space, the image resolution and the calibration used. A homography fitted for one fixed camera and pitch does not transfer to an arbitrary upload. Even within a camera profile, independent landmark residuals are needed to establish how much confidence to place in estimated metres. Measurement error propagates when successive projected positions are differenced for speed or distance. Pons *et al.* (2019) show why agreement between established positioning technologies cannot simply be presumed; a single-camera estimate warrants at least as much caution.

The appropriate design consequence is conditional measurement. Valid calibration can enable qualified spatial estimates; absent or invalid calibration leaves image-space observations without metre or kilometre-per-hour labels. Temporal gaps also interrupt a trajectory: joining positions across missing observations would turn uncertainty into invented movement. This separates the literature's mathematical possibility of projection from the evidential permission to report a particular player's physical metric.

## 2.6 Speech recognition as coaching context

Automatic speech recognition offers a time-stamped route from coaching audio to possible tactical instructions. Whisper's large-scale weakly supervised training demonstrated broad speech-recognition capability (Radford *et al.*, 2023). Efficient alternatives include Fast Conformer's redesigned attention and downsampling (Rekesh *et al.*, 2023) and token-and-duration transducers, which can skip input frames during decoding (Xu *et al.*, 2023). These model families have different accuracy, latency and resource profiles, so an application choice must consider the deployment setting as well as generic speech benchmarks.

Football training audio introduces distant speech, wind, whistles, overlapping voices and short commands that are easily confused. Word error rate measures transcript edits, but the downstream task asks whether a **tactical event** was detected with the correct category and time. A recogniser can make few overall word errors while missing a crucial trigger; conversely, a transcript may contain harmless mistakes yet preserve the instruction. Evaluation should therefore pair transcription measures with event precision, recall and timing checks. A deterministic trigger taxonomy also makes extraction auditable. Recognition of “press” or “hold” does not establish which player was addressed, nor does an unresolved target permit an invented identity. Audio contributes intent and timing; the video must supply observable response evidence.

Timestamp quality matters independently of the words. Voice-activity segmentation and decoding can shift the boundaries of a short command, changing which subsequent video frames fall into its response window. Multiple speakers make the intended addressee still less certain. The extraction layer should preserve its timestamps and uncertainty instead of converting an ambiguous utterance into a player-specific target.

## 2.7 Grounded language-model reporting

Language models can turn structured evidence into readable coaching summaries, but fluent text is not proof of factual support. FActScore evaluates long-form generation by checking atomic claims against sources, illustrating how supported and unsupported statements can coexist in one plausible response (Min *et al.*, 2023). Open model families such as Llama 3 and Qwen3 provide candidates for local generation (Llama Team, AI at Meta, 2024; Yang *et al.*, 2025); their general capabilities do not remove the need for application-specific constraints.

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is a useful conceptual precedent because it conditions generation on external evidence rather than model parameters alone (Lewis *et al.*, 2020). **This project is not a RAG system:** it does not retrieve documents from a knowledge index to answer questions. Its grounding source is a structured record of observations, timestamps, permitted metrics and explicit limitations from the analysis pipeline. That difference matters because the principal risk is not missing encyclopaedic knowledge, but a report claiming that an unidentified player obeyed a command or travelled a metric distance without valid evidence.

Evidence-linked fields, constrained output schemas and post-generation validation can make such claims checkable. A deterministic fallback is needed when a model output fails validation; a persuasive paragraph cannot override an identity or calibration gate. This shifts the language model's role from discovering facts to expressing already authorised findings. The Evaluation chapter must test both readable output and whether the generated claims remain supported.

## 2.8 Evaluation measures and the integrated gap

Tracking metrics answer different questions. CLEAR MOT's MOTA combines false positives, false negatives and identity switches, so detection errors can dominate its value (Bernardin and Stiefelhagen, 2008). IDF1 measures identity consistency through globally matched identity precision and recall (Ristani *et al.*, 2016). HOTA balances detection and association and decomposes them into DetA and AssA, making a strong detector with weak identity association easier to recognise (Luiten *et al.*, 2021). No single score establishes that a player can safely be assessed across a whole session: counts of missed objects, switches, fragmentation and the particular evaluation time span also matter.

The same principle extends beyond tracking. Transcript error does not by itself measure tactical-event extraction; reprojection accuracy does not grant an unknown camera metric validity; fluent prose does not prove that report claims follow from evidence. System evaluation must therefore inspect component accuracy, temporal alignment, safety-gate decisions and the usefulness of the resulting interface. Practitioner evidence also argues for presenting limitations in language coaches can interpret (Davidson *et al.*, 2024).

The broader literature supplies strong component ideas but leaves a system-level question for training footage. Detectors provide candidate players; trackers and ReID attempt continuity; calibration supplies conditional spatial units; ASR supplies timed coaching context; language models can express results. Each step can fail independently, and an apparently successful downstream result may conceal an unsupported upstream assumption. The research gap addressed here is the **orchestration and evaluation of these components as one evidence-controlled workflow**: linking a coach's instruction to observable visual response, while withholding player identity, metric distance or tactical compliance when the necessary evidence is missing. Chapter 3 therefore treats uncertainty and reporting permissions as architectural requirements, not merely caveats added after model selection.
