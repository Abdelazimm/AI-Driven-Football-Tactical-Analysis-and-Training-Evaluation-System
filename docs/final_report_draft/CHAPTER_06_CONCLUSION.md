# Chapter 6 — Conclusion (607 words)

## 6.1 Answer to the research question

I began with a coaching-observation problem: one person cannot watch every player, tactical instruction and response throughout a training session. The preliminary prototype showed that a model could locate people in a football image, but it could not connect an instruction to subsequent play. The final research question asked whether video, timestamped coach speech and language-based reporting could be orchestrated into an evidence-based training review **while withholding conclusions when identity, calibration or visual evidence was insufficient**. The answer is **yes, within those limits**. The completed product processes a full recorded session, identifies tactical events, aligns them with scoped visual evidence and presents a report and video review workflow. It also makes uncertainty operational: player-specific conclusions, unsupported physical units and unobserved responses are withheld. It does not diagnose motivation or provide dependable automated individual effort scores. Its contribution is a review aid whose claims are bounded by what the recording and validation actually support.

## 6.2 Main contributions

The route from the first prototype to that result required more than adding models in sequence. I compared three complete Vision methods, investigated the detector transfer and identity failures they exposed, and selected M2 as the strongest observed whole-system method without treating selection as proof of persistent identity. I compared ASR candidates using both transcript and tactical-event measures, then used a fixed post-instruction window to combine speech and visual observations. A camera-specific homography was independently checked, with metric output governed by calibration and identity permissions. Structured evidence, an eight-gate report validator and deterministic fallback made reporting inspectable when generation could not be trusted.

I then carried these decisions into a working React, FastAPI, Supabase and Modal product. The full-session run exercised upload, remote analysis, persistence and review while retaining its identity, calibration and observation limitations. A manually verified showcase demonstrated downstream tactical possibilities under stronger evidence, separately from the automated result. Finally, coach feedback led to two focused interface passes prioritising report navigation and clearer uncertainty wording. The result is both a comparative investigation and an evaluated software system.

## 6.3 What the project taught me

The hardest problem was **trustworthy evidence attribution**, rather than locating players in individual frames. A track number can survive an occlusion yet refer to a different person; a pitch coordinate can carry metres only for a validated camera; an empty response window cannot tell us that nobody responded. My early plan treated individual movement and effort indicators as a natural next step after detection. The experiments required me to revise that plan and encode a stricter rule: report only what each evidence source permits. Failed gates, missing values and model provenance therefore became part of the implementation. The coach study added another lesson: a correct withholding decision still needs a clear explanation for its user.

## 6.4 Future work

The first research priority is safer persistent identity, tested across longer occlusions, re-entry and same-team interactions with independent ground truth before any individual longitudinal claim is enabled. Calibration should become practical for new cameras through guided landmarks and separate validation; merely solving a homography should not grant metric units. The full-session runtime and missing observation windows call for profiling, stronger coverage checks and more efficient inference. Short event clips or annotated overlays could reduce review time without inventing player identities.

Once the evidence gates pass, repeated-session player comparisons could be evaluated. Team-shape analysis may be explored sooner at team scope. A larger field study should test whether coaches understand withheld results and use event-linked evidence in real workflows; the six-person formative study cannot establish that. The project ends with a narrower but more defensible ambition: AI can help coaches revisit training evidence and ask better questions, while the coach remains responsible for judging players and deciding what to do next.
